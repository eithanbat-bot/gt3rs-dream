A practical solar enquiry follow-up checklist

Solar installation requests often vary significantly: a homeowner looking for backup is not the same prospect as a facilities manager assessing a commercial PV project. A useful lead-response process collects enough context to place each enquiry with the right person, without pretending that every message is a ready-to-buy opportunity.

1. Record the source and consent

Identify how the customer reached you—your website, a callback request, an inbound WhatsApp message or another channel. Keep their express communications preferences, permissions and any objections. Do not automatically add an enquiry to unrelated marketing campaigns.

2. Capture minimum qualification details

  1. Location and site type: suburb/area, commercial or residential, and rooftop, farm or ground-mounted installation.
  2. Objective: monthly cost reduction, backup power, resilience or off-grid operation.
  3. Scale: rough electricity usage, existing inverter/battery, if known, and whether the project is a new installation or an upgrade.
  4. Timing: urgent replacement, planning this quarter or early research.
  5. Next step: site survey, quote review, technical clarification or a scheduled human call.

3. Route enquiries fairly

Use a small number of honest categories: urgent technical support, commercial site assessment, residential installation, upgrade/maintenance, and general research. Prioritise based on the enquiry's explicit urgency and project requirements, rather than inferring creditworthiness or a person's financial circumstances.

Sample acknowledgement (human-reviewed)

“Thanks for contacting our team about your solar project. Could you share your suburb, whether this is for a home or business, and your main goal—backup power or reducing electricity costs? We’ll use those details to route the request appropriately.”

Adapt to your own processes and the customer's permission to receive a reply.

4. Track a clear owner and next action

Every open enquiry should have an owner, a last-response timestamp and a next action. A simple status model works: new → qualifying → assessment scheduled → quote issued → won/lost. This is workflow discipline, not an automated revenue guarantee.

5. Test with real-world edge cases

Before deploying automation, test incomplete forms, duplicated submissions, emergency service requests, unavailable calendars, opt-outs and messages that contain private data. Keep a human handoff where technical design, pricing or customer safety is involved.

6. Review actual outcomes

Track median first response time, share of enquiries with an owner, time from enquiry to quote, and quote-to-sale conversion. Compare equivalent periods, note seasonality and do not claim improvement until the data supports it.
